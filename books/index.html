Diamond Dogs, Turquoise Days by Alastair Reynolds
My rating: ★★★★☆
anthology-collectionfictionownedread-in-2026science-fiction
While these two were undeniably interesting stories I found them lacking a certain pizzazz I had been hoping for. I think I was expecting stronger ties to the rest of the RS universe, like galactic north, but that’s not the direction he wanted to take it.

I’ll start with turquoise days which I thought was the weaker of the two. Here we follow a researcher on a remote pattern juggler world when an ultra ship arrives claiming to want to look at their research. What it does very well is providing us with the most clear explanation of how the pattern jugglers behave and how interactions actually work, and most relevantly the threats that might allow for. My issue was I don’t think I fully understood the threat as well as I should have, without going into spoilers I didn’t understand the mechanism the antagonists could use to exploit the pattern jugglers to give them what they want, so I wasn’t able to understand the danger as well as I wanted to. Apart from that though the characters and concepts were engaging and it was nice to see a different angle of the universe.

Diamond dogs was quite different in this we follow Richard as he gets invited by his old friend to try to solve the mathematical murder spire with a team. That’s really all the concept you need to understand and from there we get a look into the psychology of someone willing to risk death and destruction to find out what is at the top of this mysterious tower on an empty planet. The titular diamond dogs were a fascinating body horror element that shows the lengths childe would go to. Interestingly this also referenced the pattern jugglers specifically as being able to make you a maths genius, which happened in turquoise days too, thought that was an odd coincidence that I don’t think ever came up in the other stories.

Together these were two stories that I’d recommend to any sort of reader even without knowledge of the main series as it doesn’t tie in

The Day of the Triffids by John Wyndham
My rating: ★★★☆☆
classicsfictionownedread-in-2026science-fiction
This was a most puzzling read indeed, that I suppose was about as good as I was expecting even if it was very different. I think what struck me first was how insistent John seems to be that blindness is a curse that people are incapable of bearing, having the first characters our protagonist meet commit murder suicides upon their families when they realise they are all blind. Fortunately for the plot progression our main character managed to avoid going blind so he was allowed to stay alive and take us through this journey. I discovered after reading it that 28 days later takes inspiration from this novel, which makes a lot of sense as it’s basically the same plot (besides the whole blind thing). I think he does also sort of endorse polygyny so that was certainly a choice. As for the setting and circumstance, I found them to be oddly disjointed, I think I would have liked the beginning of the triffids to be tied to the ‘meteor’ in some way. Maybe there’s something beautifully realistic about there being millions of murder plants being grown just because they made good oil, but it seems a little unrealistic for the public parks to also be growing something that tries to kill you. All together an interesting read of a classic even if it wasn’t the best.

Why Don't We Just Kill The Kid in the Omelas Hole by Isabel J. Kim
My rating: ★☆☆☆☆
fictionread-in-2026
I found myself severely disappointed by this book from multiple angles and it boggles the mind how this won the nebula award.

I will focus first on its approach to the thought experiment first made in ‘The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas’ by Ursula K. Le Guin, which I can concede is an angle not covered in the original story, why not kill the child and end the suffering instead of walking away. You can get that message from the title which I think is a good place to stop, as through the narrative Kim seems to have ignored the original story entirely. Most glaringly to me is how she starts talking about ‘the people with the big houses’ as some over class controlling Omelas, which goes against the egalitarian society we are shown in the original. This annoyed me so much that I did actually go back to read the original and it became even worse for me: Le Guin tells us ‘so they also got on without the stock exchange’ and yet Kim dares to say ‘and the stock market started shuddering downward’ after the kid dies. It’s a five page story with very little details so I don’t understand how she can contradict it so blatantly. I think perhaps she didn’t even read it.

This might come down to more an interpretation thing but I believe the very point of the thought experiment is that everyone is equal in this society except for the single child who must suffer, I don’t think it works as strongly if you propose there’s inequality between the read of the people. Back to the actual question posed by the title though I don’t find that it truly talks to the thought experiment at all and is being used more as a vessel to make complaints about the real world without engaging philosophically with the core of Omelas.

If you read the original you might be saying how does it complain about the real world when the original text so strongly talks about how it’s far removed from everything we could know and imagine, and now we can go into the prose and how far it differs from that too. I mentioned above about the stock exchange but she also declares they actively are using social media and then compares things to being viral like Kim Kardashian’s sex tape. To cut a long story short she finds a way to reference as much modern technology as possible and to date it singularly to now, lacking the agelessness so central to Omelas. You can’t have a fairy tale set in the current era it just doesn’t work.

Finally the least grounded but probably most irritating was just the way it was written, it felt like a tumblr post or some offer modern way of speaking that just didn’t align with the tone of the narrative at all. It could almost read like a satire of how a millennial might write at times. Alongside that she uses the term ‘load-bearing suffering child’ word for word seventeen times throughout the text, I guess to try to really drive the message home but it just reeks of desperation to seem like she understands the thought experiment.

I don’t know what else to say but it’s a disservice to Ursula K. Le Guin and I cannot recommend it less

The State of the Art (Culture, #4) by Iain M. Banks

Banks has given us quite a mixed bag today, but I guess that’s the curse of a short story collection. I think my biggest complaint is that despite being a numbered book in the culture series only 3/8 stories in the collection were actually set in the culture universe. I guess I was given too much in Galactic North which gave an incredibly broad fleshing out of the universe it was set in and hoped this would be the same.

I think the best place to start is the titular novella, which covers about half of the book, in which we follow Diziet Sma (from use of weapons) on a mission with contact to Earth in the 1970s. This was immediately a fascinating choice as in my mind the culture was far future and would ever even mention earth so my interest was piqued. I think this is definitely his most clearly political story so far, directly contrasting the socialist post-scarcity existence of the culture citizens to life on earth. He repeats a line here from the first story in the collection that ‘money is a sign of poverty’, arguing that in a truly rich society everyone would have their needs met so there would be no need for money. You would think then that the story is quite simplistic, the culture looks down on earth for not being as good as them and while that is an opinion held by many of the culture citizens we do also get a far more interesting opposing opinion from a character that chooses to leave the culture and become a human forever as they find more value in that existence. His argument here is that in a society where you can live forever and always have everything you desire there’s really no point in living at all, instead he desires the uncertainly of human life as the negatives make you appreciate the good things in life far more than you ever could if you never suffered. This internal conflict of opinions in the story left me thinking about what kind of life is truly better long after I finished. Definitely a five star read for me

As for the other stories I found them each a little underwhelming. I think the strongest would have to be descendent which gives us a very internal story of a man and his suit attempting to walk back to civilisation after crash landing as we see them breaking down mentally. Unfortunately the ending was spoilt for me by the drawing underneath the title so that sort of scuppered the emotional depth of the narrative. The weakest was the final story, if you can call it that, it was a sort of stream of consciousness word babble about the future that just didn’t work for me, maybe it’s somebody’s favourite but I just couldn’t get behind it
The Lion, the Witch, and the Wardrobe (Chronicles of Narnia #2) by C.S. Lewis
My rating: ★★☆☆☆
classicsfictionhigh-fantasyownedread-in-2026
I fear this might not be a commonly held belief but I think this is fundamentally not a good book. I think there are three things that really make up a story: setting, characters and plot. Of those three I think it only succeeds at writing a setting as the plot and characters were utterly hollow. I’ll start by saying that I refute the claim that this a children’s book and can’t be held to the same standards, it’s incredibly popular and influential to children’s development so if anything it matters even more that it has strong narrative lessons.

I think I’ll start by breaking down the characters, or perhaps lack thereof. We have our four main siblings, Lucy, Edmund, Susan and Peter who can basically be described as sweet, evil, caring and leader. I will cut it a little slack that this is the first book they are in and it’s very short so any sort of character development would need to be limited but Peter and Susan’s entire arc is they don’t believe in Narnia, they go to narnia and now they are leaders of Narnia. Lucy as the most important character at least gets some emotion when no one believes her about it but then she goes back to being one note. The most egregious character of all through is Edmund who genuinely just becomes evil and starts doing insane bullshit for no discernible reason. I get maybe he fights with his siblings but that does not explain his actions at all. Then when he fails in his evil schemes he decides to give it all up and become normal again in the blink of an eye.

These one note characters are not helped at all by a plot so contrived that I don’t believe they even needed to be in the story for things to turn out exactly the same. We start out with Lucy travelling into Narnia and meeting Tumnus who initially plans to turn her in to the white queen but feels so guilty he lets her go back to the real world. When Lucy tells her siblings they of course don’t believe her so it’s not until later when Edmund follows her into the wardrobe by coincidence that he also enters Narnia meeting the white queen and being offered Turkish delights to tempt him. The queen then demands of him that he brings all the siblings in with him next time and if he does he can get even more Turkish delights and become king. When Lucy finds him afterwards she’s excited that he finally believes her so they head back to the real world. So now thinking about the fact Edmund has become addicted to Turkish delight and is willing to betray his family to the white queen that Lucy has told him is evil you would think he would try to bring his siblings with him to get everything he was promised but no because he’s now become evil he instead lies to them and says that Lucy is still lying and he never went in there. It just makes no sense are we meant to believe that he hates Lucy to such an extent that it’s more powerful than the spell he was under compelling him to bring his siblings back. Despite this setback they do eventually all go into the wardrobe to hide and end up in Narnia, immediately exposing Edmund for lying about going, which I will reiterate he had nothing to gain by doing. They go to visit Mr Tumnus who has been kidnapped after Edmund told the queen that he hadn’t turned Lucy in before, so the children instead find the beaver family who let them in and tell them that it’s all ok because Aslan has returned and he will save Narnia. In this time Edmund decides to betray his family again and sneaks out to find the queen and tell her where his siblings are and about Aslan returning. When the others find this out they immediately panic and rush to escape the house and find Aslan ASAP. Along the way the winter (which was previously eternal due to the witch’s magic) suddenly changes and Father Christmas arrives and gives them all presents, because it turns out that because Aslan is back the seasons can change again (very convenient). On the other side Edmund finds the queen and tells her everything and then she immediately kidnaps him and takes him on her sled, which promptly breaks down due to the winter ending so they need to walk to Aslan, with Edmund tied up as a prisoner. Eventually both groups end up at the stone table with Aslan and they do a trade where the witch lets Edmund go free but Aslan must die in his place as ‘The Deep Magic’ says someone must die for every betrayal and Aslan sacrifices himself on the table to let the four children become rulers of Narnia. Once this is done the witch then attacks everyone else so is now undefended. Good news through it turns out there’s even deeper magic from before the deep magic so the witch didn’t know about it and it says that if you sacrifice yourself on the stone you get resurrected so Aslan is now back and he goes to the Witch’s house to revive all the people she turned to stone and then the whole army goes back and kills the witch huzzah. This covers the first 80% of the story

That ended up being a long summary and I’m sorry but I hope you will notice something about it, the children did literally nothing except make things worse, Aslan make the winter end, Aslan revived the stone people, Aslan lead the armies, why were the children necessary to the story. After all that the children become king and rule for decades until they talk different and then one day they accidentally walk into the doorway not recognising it anymore and end up back as children in the real world and then the book stops. If you want my truth cut the first part down by 90% and give me a story about when they were rulers and more importantly the emotional impact decades of ruling a fantasy world and then coming back to the real world still a child as through no time had passed would have on you. He has written the wrong story here I don’t care about children turning up to a war that’s already about to be solved I care about how they feel after war and being forced into a position of power with no qualifications. That actually might be too much to ask for from a children’s book but I thought I’d put the idea in your head.

I’d gladly like to hear any thoughts about why my summary and analysis that the children were not relevant in their own story was wrong but really I doubt anyone is going to read this far anyway. Hope the next ones are better

From Russia with Love by Ian Fleming
My rating: ★★★★☆
fictionmysteryownedread-in-2026spyclassics
I found this to be a refreshing change of pace compared to the previous novels. It of course had the core elements, but it came at it from a different angle (literally) having the first third be entirely from SMERSH’s perspective as we get to know the antagonists and what they are planning. This provides us a with a much deeper understanding of the motivations and places them in much more of a leading role even when the chapters move to being from bond’s perspective. Find out afterwards that Fleming had become bored with writing the bond novels and planned this to be a possible finale has definitely recontextualised the breakdown of his character throughout the plot, starting with him being bored and apathetic with his job and then breaking down his flaws throughout. I still feel the need to ask how on earth James Bond could ever be a good spy is the Russians know so much about him that they have his name, picture and sexual proclivities in a file that every head has read. This leads to their genius plan being to convince an attractive Russian woman to tell the British spymaster in Turkey she’s fallen in love with James Bond solely from reading the file and wants to defect to the uk and give them the cryptography system, but only if he comes to collect her personally. Now I understand getting their hands on Russian codes is very important but I truly do wonder how the entire British spy service managed to fall for such an unbelievable story and actually send him out to do it. Even when he finds out that the Russian almost definitely planned it all, Bond decides to just ‘let it play out’. I think this was an intentional choice to show that he is getting sloppy and actually isn’t a good spy entirely because he falls in love so often. This is contrasted to the Russian assassin who is highlighted to be good at his job at the start of the novel because he is asexual and emotionally detached from the work meaning unlike other assassins he has managed to stay competent after many years of killing. I don’t think Bond is meant to have done other missions in between the novels as it’s said in this that the Russians are only aware of the ones previously novelised so if that’s to be believed he’s completed 4 missions as a double-o and already burnt out, showing a far more human side than the films. Even aside from being bad at his job I do think this has continued to highlight to me that the reader was never meant to like bond as a person, which is very important in a book like this. It makes me think of what Truffaut is supposed to have said ‘there is no such thing as an anti-war film’ and I think it’s true for spy books/films too, Fleming in part attempted to show an unlikeable depiction of spycraft but in the end it’s been adapted to perhaps the most romanticised spy films there are.

Klara and the Sun by Kazuo Ishiguro
My rating: ★★★☆☆
fictionownedscience-fictiondystopiaread-in-2026
I'm sad to say that I found this to be a very disappointing novel. As the first novel after he won the nobel prize I had expected something groundbreaking and yet I think in the end it felt like an echo of Never Let Me Go. I'm not going to go so far as to say it is a bad book but it just felt to me as though he put all the necessary parts of a good novel in and then left out the spark to ignite it and bring it all together.

It had a strong concept that was especially relevant when it came out: during the lockdowns, necessity of education being at home and ensuing loneliness that would come out of it. I think it was able to act well upon this concept for the first half with the socalisation events and need for a friend like Klara or Rick and yet as it went further along I think the core themes got muddied by the introduction of increasing amounts of tangents. At first they add the concept of being lifted, tying into the illness and creating the sideplot of Rick's struggle to get into school despite it. Then they get information about the portrait and how that ties into the illness as well and Klara's role and from there I felt as though Kazuo didn't know what he wanted the story to be as each of the plots fought to be relevant and yet each ends up unsatisfied. I can understand that in a novel like this the message is often more important than the plot so it could be forgiven, and yet the ending seems to go against what I thought the message had been the whole time so then I was left with nothing at all.
